Where the Noise Comes From

A THz waveform's noise is not one number and it is not flat. It has three physical sources that live in different places on the pulse, and telling them apart says which part of the instrument is limiting you. This walks through that measurement on a real CNT acquisition, and shows why the noise floor we had been quoting was measuring something else entirely.

The three sources

Every measured waveform is the true pulse plus a noise term whose size changes along the record. The model splits that size into three contributions, each with a different physical origin and a different signature in time:

σ²(t) = σα² + [σβ·μ(t)]² + [στ·μ̇(t)]²

They are separable precisely because they sit in different places: the additive term is the only one present where there is no pulse, the multiplicative term peaks where the pulse peaks, and the jitter term peaks on the pulse's steep flanks where a small timing error produces a large amplitude error.

Reading these numbers

The three amplitudes are not directly comparable — they carry different units. What makes them comparable is how much variance each contributes over the whole record: {{SHARE_ADD}}% additive, {{SHARE_MULT}}% multiplicative, {{SHARE_JIT}}% jitter for this acquisition.

Jitter dominating is a statement about this pulse as much as this instrument: a {{TAU}} fs timing error is tiny, but the CNT pulse is steep, so μ̇ is large and a tiny time error becomes a sizeable amplitude error.

Step 1

What we are actually working with

The pipeline consumes one averaged waveform. But the instrument recorded {{N_SCANS}} separate scans over {{MINUTES}} minutes, and the file keeps every one of them. The spread between those scans is a direct measurement of the noise — it is already in the data, and until now we were averaging it away and then trying to recover it from the spectrum instead.

Where this one does not fit yet

Look at the two broad shoulders in the measured curve around 109.4 and 109.9 ps: the model under-predicts them, while over-predicting the single narrow central spike. The most likely explanation is that adjacent time samples are correlated by the lock-in time constant, which breaks the model's assumption that each sample's noise is independent. That is worth chasing, and it does not affect the measured σ(t) curve itself — only the three-way decomposition of it.

Step 4

The instrument does not sit still

Each scan carries a wall-clock timestamp, so the fitted per-scan amplitude and arrival time can be plotted against real elapsed time. Over {{MINUTES}} minutes the amplitude climbs {{DRIFT_AMP}}% and the pulse arrives {{DRIFT_DELAY}} fs later, both with the saturating shape of a system settling toward equilibrium.

This is the purge box coming to equilibrium — the signature already identified in the lab notebook. Falling water vapour raises the transmitted amplitude; the air-to-nitrogen refractive index change shifts the arrival time. What is new here is that the noise estimator recovers it for free, as a by-product of doing its job properly.

Step 5

Drift is not noise

That drift is a problem for a naive noise estimate. If you simply take the spread across scans, a pulse that grew 5% over the run looks like a very noisy pulse. The estimator therefore fits out each scan's amplitude and delay first, and measures the scatter of what remains.

Counting drift as noise would have inflated the estimate {{INFLATION}}× on this acquisition.

Two things worth being clear about. The correction happens inside the estimator — the averaged waveform the pipeline analyses is never modified, so this is a diagnostic decomposition and not a silent fix applied to your data. And drift being separated out does not make it harmless: it is a real systematic that averaging does not remove, which is why it is reported rather than discarded.

Step 6

Into the frequency domain

The measured σ(t) is pushed through the same window and DFT the pipeline uses, giving a genuine per-bin uncertainty on the spectrum. Comparing it against the tail-median "noise floor" we had been quoting shows the size of the problem.

The conventional floor sits {{FLOOR_RATIO}}× above the measured uncertainty.

The floor is the median of |Y| over the top quarter of the frequency axis. That is only a noise measurement if the spectrum has flattened into a noise plateau up there. On a short record with a sharp pulse it has not — there is still real pulse content at the sampling limit — so the number that comes back is set by the signal, not the noise.

Step 7

Which estimator is measuring noise?

There is a clean test that needs no theory. Averaging fewer scans is more noise, by a known amount: uncertainty must fall as 1/√M. So compute both estimates from the first M scans for increasing M. Multiply each by √M and anything that is genuinely measuring noise will hold flat.

This is the direct explanation for something that had been puzzling: improving the instrument's noise never seemed to shrink the error bars. It could not. The quantity being reported was not responding to noise at all.

Step 8

What actually limits this measurement

With an honest noise number, the interesting question becomes what dominates. Compare the within-scan noise against a batch-means estimate that also sees the slow drift.

That is an actionable result and it points somewhere specific. Since the drift here is the purge settling, the fix is not a better detector or more scans — it is waiting for equilibrium, or interleaving sample and reference so the drift affects both equally.

What this does not tell you

  • The decomposition is not yet a good fit. Profile mismatch of {{PROFILE_ERR}} against an expected {{PROFILE_EXPECTED}} means the three-term model is missing something — most likely correlation between adjacent time samples from the lock-in. The total σ(t) is measured, not modelled, so error bars built from it are unaffected; it is the attribution between the three sources that carries the caveat.
  • Per-bin error bars cannot express frequency correlation. Because σ(t) varies with time, its transform enters the spectrum as a convolution, correlating neighbouring bins. The bars shown are the correct marginals for any single bin and are mildly pessimistic for a feature spanning several.
  • Below about eight repeats, the estimate is unreliable. A variance estimate from M samples carries a relative error of 1/√(2(M−1)) — roughly 40% at M = 4. This depends only on the number of repeats, not on how noisy each one is.
  • Nothing here is wired into the pipeline yet. These numbers come from the raw .acc files. Connecting the estimator to the processing chain needs the per-scan matrix to survive the first pipeline step, which it currently does not.
